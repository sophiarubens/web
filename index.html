Sophia Rubens

Instrument-aware theoretical cosmology graduate student

I began my PhD work in the McGill Physics Department and Trottier Space Institute in August 2024.

Most of my work so far has been simulating how realistic instrumental systematics and astrophysical foregrounds will compromise our ability to extract meaningful cosmological conclusions from post-reionization 21 cm intensity maps prepared using the under-construction Canadian Hydrogen Observatory and Radio-transient Detector (CHORD). My pipeline is end-to-end, meaning I simulate the sky from an input model, modulate it by the instrumental response, and form power spectra—the ubiquitous cosmological summary statistic for showing how a signal (in this case, neutral hydrogen) concentrates on different spatial scales. Unlike typical simulations of this variety, I use beams from electromagnetic simulations—which encode more realistic features than most theory models—and give different antennas in the CHORD array different systematics, which is an experimental reality but not often simulated

Recently, I've embarked upon a new project in parallel. The CHORD collaboration is measuring the instrument's beam (that is, its sensitivity to different parts of the sky) using transits of point sources. We can't change the number of bright radio point sources in the northern sky, but by motorizing the elevation angle axis of a single antenna, we can use convenient properties of radio interferometers to generate a more complete beam map—especially after building upon our measurements using Bayesian inference. I'm simulating what CHORD will see for several possible motor designs to get a sense for which case will facilitate the best inference for the most reasonable hardware investment. Looking ahead, I'm eager to branch out into star-formation line intensity mapping and observation-simulation synergies related to the epoch of reionization.
